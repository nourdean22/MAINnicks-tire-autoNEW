## PART A — EVIDENCE TABLE

### 1a. Interruption cost & resumption lag — field measurement
- **Claim:** Interrupted tasks are not resumed immediately; workers pass through multiple other tasks before returning, and the average "resumption lag" is large (order of tens of minutes) in real office settings.
- **Primary source:** Mark, G., Gonzalez, V.M., & Harris, J. (2005). "No Task Left Behind? Examining the Nature of Fragmented Work." Proc. CHI 2005, ACM, 321-330. DOI: 10.1145/1054972.1055017. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1054972.1055017 (PDF: https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/CHI2005.pdf)
- **Effect size / key number:** 77% of interrupted tasks resumed same day; ~25 minutes average elapsed before resumption, with >2 intervening tasks typically completed first; internally-initiated interruptions added ~6.5 more minutes to resumption than externally-initiated ones.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (single observational field study of a couple dozen information workers; frequently over-generalized in popular press as "it takes 23 minutes to refocus," which the study does not claim — it measured calendar-time-to-return-to-task amid other legitimate work, not attention-recovery time).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Treat "task resumption" as a first-class object the OS manages (saved context/state per task), not something the operator's memory is expected to hold across a 25-minute gap; surface "what was I doing" context automatically when a task is reopened.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do NOT conclude interruptions themselves cost 23-25 minutes of lost work — that number is elapsed calendar time to circle back amid legitimate intervening tasks, not a measure of wasted or unproductive time.

### 1b. Interruption cost & resumption lag — instrumented desktop study
- **Claim:** In logged field data, the visibility of a suspended task's window/artifacts and whether an interruption was self-initiated vs. externally alerted both significantly affect how quickly a task is resumed.
- **Primary source:** Iqbal, S.T. & Horvitz, E. (2007). "Disruption and Recovery of Computing Tasks: Field Study, Analysis, and Directions." Proc. CHI 2007, ACM, 677-686. DOI: 10.1145/1240624.1240730. https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/CHI_2007_Iqbal_Horvitz-1.pdf
- **Effect size / key number:** No single top-line effect size; core finding is that resumption speed is a function of window visibility and interruption type (external alert vs. self-initiated), with self-diversions showing delayed re-engagement patterns distinct from alert-driven ones.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (single instrumented field study, real-world log data, not independently replicated at the same granularity, but methodologically strong and widely cited).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Keep suspended-task artifacts (drafts, open threads, partial forms) visually persistent/one-tap-reachable rather than fully closing views; distinguish "operator tapped away" from "OS pushed an alert" when deciding how much resumption scaffolding to show.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not assume all interruptions are equally costly — the paper's point is that context (visibility, interruption source) moderates cost, so blanket minimize-all-interruptions policies are not what the data supports.

### 1c. Interruption cost — memory-for-goals theoretical model
- **Claim:** People resume interrupted tasks by re-activating a decaying mental "goal" trace formed at the moment of suspension; longer/more demanding interruptions degrade this trace more, producing longer resumption times, and rehearsal at suspension mitigates the decay.
- **Primary source:** Altmann, E.M. & Trafton, J.G. (2002). "Memory for Goals: An Activation-Based Model." Cognitive Science, 26(1), 39-83. DOI: 10.1207/s15516709cog2601_2. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15516709cog2601_2 (follow-up experimental test: Altmann & Trafton, 2007, "Timecourse of Recovery from Task Interruption," Psychonomic Bulletin & Review.)
- **Effect size / key number:** Model plus experiments show resumption time increases with interruption duration and interruption demand (interaction effect), consistent with activation decay; no single universal effect size (it is a process model fit to multiple datasets).
- **Grade:** ROBUST (originating Cognitive Science paper plus multiple independent experimental tests by the same and other labs over 2000s-2010s converge on the activation/decay account; widely used as the standard computational model of interruption in HCI).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Prompt the operator to externalize a one-line "where I was / what's next" note at the moment of suspension (voluntary goal rehearsal) — the model predicts this measurably speeds resumption; auto-capturing the last app state serves the same function without operator effort.
- **What NOT to conclude:** This is a cognitive model of individual memory decay, not a claim about organizational productivity loss — do not stretch it into ROI/dollar-cost estimates, which require separate and weaker evidence.

### 2a. Task-switching cost — review of the "switch cost" literature
- **Claim:** Response times and error rates reliably increase immediately after switching tasks (vs. repeating the same task), even with advance preparation time; a residual cost persists no matter how much preparation is allowed.
- **Primary source:** Monsell, S. (2003). "Task Switching." Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 7(3), 134-140. DOI: 10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7. https://www.cell.com/trends/cognitive-sciences/abstract/S1364-6613(03)00028-7
- **Effect size / key number:** Review reports switch costs typically in the range of tens to a few hundred milliseconds per trial in lab paradigms (task-dependent); costs are reduced but not eliminated by preparation ("residual switch cost").
- **Grade:** ROBUST (review article synthesizing a large, highly replicated experimental-psychology literature spanning decades).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Group same-type micro-actions (all approvals, then all replies, then all scheduling) into short batched sessions inside the OS rather than interleaving task types on every screen; expect a small but real "re-entry tax" every time the operator context-switches between domains (finance vs. ops vs. marketing).
- **What NOT to conclude:** Lab switch-cost numbers (hundreds of ms per trial) do NOT directly translate into the popularized "you lose 40% of your productive time to multitasking" claim — that figure is a misattribution/overgeneralization, not a finding of this literature (see 2b).

### 2b. Task-switching cost — dual-task/rule-activation experiments
- **Claim:** Alternating between two task rules costs measurable time beyond doing either task repeatedly, with the cost driven by "goal shifting" and "rule activation" processing stages; the size of the cost grows with rule complexity and shrinks with advance cuing.
- **Primary source:** Rubinstein, J.S., Meyer, D.E., & Evans, J.E. (2001). "Executive Control of Cognitive Processes in Task Switching." Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 27(4), 763-797. DOI: 10.1037/0096-1523.27.4.763. https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/xhp274763.pdf
- **Effect size / key number:** Four experiments; switching time costs on the order of a few hundred milliseconds to roughly 1-2 seconds per switch depending on rule complexity/familiarity, not a percentage of total time budget.
- **Grade:** ROBUST (peer-reviewed, four converging experiments, foundational and heavily replicated in the two decades since).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Where the OS asks the operator to alternate between structurally different judgment types (e.g., approve a numeric budget vs. approve creative copy) back-to-back, expect slower/more error-prone responses on the first item after the switch; sequence similar decision types together.
- **What NOT to conclude:** This paper is frequently cited without citation for the "multitasking costs you 40% of your time" soundbite — the actual experiments measured sub-second-to-low-second RT costs per switch in artificial lab tasks, not a general 40%-productivity-loss figure; that number should be treated as NOT VERIFIED / folk statistic.

### 3a. Prospective memory — event-based cues beat time-based recall
- **Claim:** Remembering to perform a deferred intention ("prospective memory") is a distinct memory system from remembering facts, and it is substantially easier when supported by a salient external event-cue than when it depends on self-initiated monitoring of time passing.
- **Primary source:** Einstein, G.O. & McDaniel, M.A. (1990). "Normal Aging and Prospective Memory." Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 16(4), 717-726. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2142956/ (event- vs. time-based distinction developed further across the Einstein & McDaniel Multiprocess Theory research program through the 1990s-2000s.)
- **Effect size / key number:** No single universal number; the durable, replicated qualitative finding across the program is that time-based PM shows larger performance costs / more self-initiated-monitoring demand than event-based PM, and that low cue-salience or high focal-task load both degrade PM performance.
- **Grade:** ROBUST (large, multi-decade replicated experimental literature; Einstein & McDaniel's Multiprocess Theory is a standard reference framework in cognitive psychology).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Prefer event-triggered reminders ("when the invoice arrives," "when you open the shop app") over pure clock-time reminders ("at 3pm, remember to...") wherever a natural trigger event exists; when only time-based reminding is possible, increase cue salience (interruptive, not just a badge) because it is the harder memory mode.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude that more reminders are always better — the same literature (and applied notification research, see topic 15) shows high reminder volume degrades the salience/distinctiveness that makes event-based cues work in the first place.

### 3b. Implementation intentions — if-then planning meta-analysis
- **Claim:** Forming a specific if-then plan ("if situation Y arises, I will do X") substantially increases the rate at which people actually follow through on goals, compared with merely holding a goal intention.
- **Primary source:** Gollwitzer, P.M. & Sheeran, P. (2006). "Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-Analysis of Effects and Processes." Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69-119. DOI: 10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/chapter/bookseries/abs/pii/S0065260106380021
- **Effect size / key number:** d = 0.65 (medium-to-large effect) on goal attainment, aggregated across 94 independent tests/studies.
- **Grade:** ROBUST (meta-analysis of 94 independent studies; one of the more robust effects in applied social/cognitive psychology, though later replication-era re-analyses suggest some publication-bias-adjusted shrinkage — direction of effect still holds).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** When the operator sets a task/goal, prompt for the specific trigger condition and action ("when X happens, I will do Y") rather than a bare due date — this is one of the highest-confidence, cheapest-to-implement mechanisms available for the whole report.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not assume implementation intentions help equally for all goal types — the meta-analysis shows stronger effects for simple/well-defined goals than for very complex, multi-step, novel goals, and effects are smaller when people start low in goal commitment.

### 4a. Distributed cognition — the system, not the individual, is the cognitive unit
- **Claim:** In complex real-world work (the case study is naval-ship navigation), no single person holds the complete knowledge/procedure needed; cognition (memory, computation, tracking) is distributed across people, instruments, and representations, and the socio-technical assembly as a whole is the unit that "thinks."
- **Primary source:** Hutchins, E. (1995). Cognition in the Wild. MIT Press. ISBN 9780262581462. https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262581462/cognition-in-the-wild/ (see also Hutchins, E., "Distributed Cognition" summary: https://arl.human.cornell.edu/linked%20docs/Hutchins_Distributed_Cognition.pdf)
- **Effect size / key number:** N/A — qualitative ethnographic monograph, not an effect-size study.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (single, deeply influential ethnographic case study; the broader distributed-cognition paradigm it launched has since been extended by convergent qualitative work in aviation, air-traffic control, and surgical teams, but that is convergent qualitative replication, not RCT-style replication).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Design the OS as external memory/coordination infrastructure the operator thinks WITH (shared state, checklists, logs that persist the "why" of past decisions), not merely a tool the operator thinks ABOUT — a single-operator business is itself a distributed system across the operator, staff, vendors, and the OS.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude that adding more external artifacts/tools automatically improves cognition — Hutchins's cases work because representations are well-matched to task structure; a poorly designed external representation can add coordination overhead instead of reducing it.

### 4b. External cognition — graphical representations reshape the task, not just display it
- **Claim:** Graphical/external representations aid thinking not merely by storing information outside the head, but by re-structuring the cognitive task itself (making relations directly perceivable, constraining possible next actions, offloading computation onto the representation).
- **Primary source:** Scaife, M. & Rogers, Y. (1996). "External Cognition: How Do Graphical Representations Work?" International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 45(2), 185-213. DOI: 10.1006/ijhc.1996.0048. https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp335.pdf
- **Effect size / key number:** N/A — theoretical/synthesis paper proposing constructs (computational offloading, re-representation, graphical constraining); the paper itself states the empirical evidence base for why graphics help was fragmented/poorly understood at the time of writing.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (foundational, extremely widely cited framework paper, but explicitly a conceptual synthesis rather than a new controlled experiment; treat as theory-grade, not effect-size-grade).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** When showing the operator status/progress (revenue, task queues, ad performance), prefer representations that let a relation be read off directly (e.g., a queue length as a visibly shrinking bar) over ones that require mental computation from raw numbers — the representation should do part of the reasoning.
- **What NOT to conclude:** The existence of a chart or dashboard widget does not by itself guarantee a cognitive benefit — the paper's own caveat is that graphical benefit depends on a good match between representation structure and task structure, which must be evaluated per widget, not assumed.

### 5. Recognition rather than recall
- **Claim:** People retrieve information faster and more accurately when the interface lets them recognize a previously-seen option (cued retrieval) than when it requires unaided recall (generating the answer from memory with no cue); interfaces that expose choices rather than demanding memorized commands/names reduce cognitive load.
- **Primary source:** Underlying memory-science base: Tulving, E. (1983). Elements of Episodic Memory. Oxford University Press (recognition-vs-recall literature broadly, including Mandler, G. (1980), "Recognizing: The Judgment of Previous Occurrence," Psychological Review, 87(3), 252-271). HCI codification: Nielsen, J. (1994), "Enhancing the Explanatory Power of Usability Heuristics," derived from a factor analysis of 249 usability problems; listed today as Heuristic #6, "Recognition rather than recall." https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/ ; https://www.nngroup.com/articles/recognition-and-recall/
- **Effect size / key number:** No single unified effect size; the underlying recognition-vs-recall memory asymmetry is one of the most replicated basic findings in cognitive psychology (recognition accuracy routinely far exceeds free-recall accuracy for the same material across hundreds of studies).
- **Grade:** ROBUST for the underlying cognitive-psychology phenomenon (recognition beats recall); PLAUSIBLE for its specific codification as a usability heuristic (practitioner-derived from a problem-corpus factor analysis, not itself a controlled A/B study).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Favor tappable lists of likely next actions, recent items, and search-with-autocomplete over blank command inputs or "remember the right menu path" navigation — especially important on a phone where recall under thumb-typing is costlier than on desktop.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude recall-driven interfaces (command palettes, natural-language input) are bad — for an expert power-user operating their own OS, a well-cued recall interface (see Raycast, Part B) can be faster than menu-recognition once the operator has learned the vocabulary; the heuristic is about novice/occasional-use load, not expert throughput.

### 6. Progressive disclosure — a design pattern with thin direct evidence
- **Claim:** Deferring secondary options/detail to a subsidiary view, showing only primary options by default, reduces error and complexity for users versus showing everything at once.
- **Primary source:** Pattern named/popularized by Nielsen, J. (1995), "Progressive Disclosure," in Usability Engineering, and later NN/G articles: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/ . Secondary sources in this literature note that Carroll, J.M. & Rosson, M.B. (minimal-manual tradition) originally observed a lack of controlled empirical evidence for the pattern's effectiveness.
- **Effect size / key number:** NOT VERIFIED — no controlled experimental effect size could be confirmed; the original Carroll & Rosson "no empirical evidence exists" statement resisted direct primary-source retrieval in this session (found only via secondary NN/G-adjacent citation). NN/G's own supporting evidence is qualitative case studies (e.g., a hotel-booking single-screen-vs-multistep usability test) rather than a published effect size.
- **Grade:** SPECULATIVE-to-PLAUSIBLE (widely adopted best-practice pattern with credible practitioner case studies from NN/G, but the pattern's own literature flags that rigorous controlled-experiment evidence is scarce; treat as design consensus, not settled science).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Use progressive disclosure for genuinely secondary/rare settings (advanced automation rules, raw logs) but do not treat it as a proven productivity lever for primary, frequently used flows — validate with StateNour's own usage data rather than citing the pattern as self-justifying.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not cite "progressive disclosure is proven to reduce cognitive load" as an established experimental fact — the honest state of evidence is expert consensus/case-study level, one notch below claims like implementation intentions or switch costs.

### 7a. Automation complacency and automation bias — integrative review
- **Claim:** When people work alongside automation/decision aids under multi-task load, they under-monitor the automation (complacency) and over-rely on its suggestions even when wrong (bias), producing both omission errors (missing a problem automation did not flag) and commission errors (acting on a wrong automated suggestion); this occurs in both novices and experts and is not fixed by simple practice or generic training.
- **Primary source:** Parasuraman, R. & Manzey, D.H. (2010). "Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation: An Attentional Integration." Human Factors, 52(3), 381-410. DOI: 10.1177/0018720810376055. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720810376055
- **Effect size / key number:** Integrative review of the empirical literature rather than a single effect size; consistent qualitative finding across reviewed studies that complacency/bias effects persist across naive and expert samples and resist simple practice-based fixes.
- **Grade:** ROBUST (systematic integrative review synthesizing many independently replicated empirical studies of complacency/bias across aviation, process control, and decision-aid domains).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Never present an AI suggestion as a silent auto-applied default in a multi-task context (e.g., while the operator is also texting a customer) — force a lightweight but real attention-check (show the AI's specific numbers, not just "Approve?") especially for judgment-relevant actions.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude "more training will fix over-reliance" — the review specifically finds practice/training alone does not reliably reduce complacency or bias; structural interface/accountability changes are needed instead (see 7b).

### 7b. Automation bias — accountability shapes verification behavior
- **Claim:** Decision-makers who are made accountable in advance for the accuracy of their decisions (not just for following procedure) commit significantly fewer automation-bias errors (both omission and commission) than non-accountable decision-makers when working with an imperfect automated aid.
- **Primary source:** Skitka, L.J., Mosier, K.L., & Burdick, M.D. (1999). "Does Automation Bias Decision-Making?" International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 51(5), 991-1006. DOI: 10.1006/ijhc.1999.0252. https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1006/ijhc.1999.0252 (companion: Mosier, K.L., Skitka, L.J., Heers, S., & Burdick, M. (1998), "Automation Bias: Decision Making and Performance in High-Tech Cockpits," International Journal of Aviation Psychology, 8(1), 47-63.)
- **Effect size / key number:** Pre-decisional accountability manipulation produced significantly lower omission- and commission-error rates vs. a non-accountable control condition in a simulated flight task; exact percentage-point deltas NOT VERIFIED in this session — treat magnitude as unconfirmed, direction/significance as PLAUSIBLE-confirmed.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (small cluster of related lab/simulation studies from one overlapping research group; conceptually corroborated by the broader review in 7a but not independently replicated at scale by unrelated labs to a confirmed degree in this session).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Build a lightweight "accountability moment" before high-stakes AI-suggested actions fire (a receipt-style summary the operator must actively review/tap, not a pre-checked default) — this is the mechanistic reason Claude Code's permission prompts and receipts (Part B) are evidence-aligned, not just UX polish.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not over-engineer accountability friction into every low-stakes action — the studies target high-stakes, error-consequential decisions (cockpit-equivalent); applying heavy friction to trivial actions will just train the operator to click through it, defeating the mechanism.

### 8. Trust calibration — trust should track actual capability, not be maximized
- **Claim:** Trust in automation should be "calibrated" (matched to the automation's real capability) rather than simply maximized; miscalibration in either direction — overtrust (leading to misuse/complacency) or undertrust (leading to disuse of a genuinely capable tool) — degrades joint human-automation performance, and design features (transparency of process, feedback about reliability, understanding of purpose) shape calibration.
- **Primary source:** Lee, J.D. & See, K.A. (2004). "Trust in Automation: Designing for Appropriate Reliance." Human Factors, 46(1), 50-80. DOI: 10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392
- **Effect size / key number:** N/A — integrative conceptual/theoretical model paper synthesizing prior empirical trust-automation literature, not a single new effect size.
- **Grade:** ROBUST (extremely widely cited synthesis grounded in a large pre-existing empirical base; the "calibration, resolution, specificity" trust framework it proposes has since been used and tested in dozens of subsequent empirical studies).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Give the operator ongoing, concrete feedback about where the AI is reliable vs. weak (e.g., "I'm confident on scheduling, uncertain on legal wording") rather than a uniform confident tone everywhere — miscalibrated blanket confidence is what produces both overtrust and, once broken, overcorrected distrust.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not treat "increase trust" as an unconditional design goal — per this framework, driving trust higher than warranted is itself a design failure mode (it predicts overtrust/complacency), not a success.

### 9. Explanation effects and their limits — explanations increase acceptance, not necessarily accuracy
- **Claim:** In human-AI decision teams, adding an explanation to an AI's recommendation increases the likelihood a person accepts that recommendation, but this acceptance increase happens regardless of whether the recommendation is correct — so explanations do not reliably produce complementary team performance (team better than either alone) and can instead just increase reliance.
- **Primary source:** Bansal, G., Wu, T., Zhou, J., Fok, R., Nushi, B., Kamar, E., Ribeiro, M.T., & Weld, D. (2021). "Does the Whole Exceed its Parts? The Effect of AI Explanations on Complementary Team Performance." Proc. CHI 2021, ACM. DOI: 10.1145/3411764.3445717. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3411764.3445717
- **Effect size / key number:** Mixed-methods studies across 3 datasets; explanations increased recommendation-acceptance rates but did not increase complementary team accuracy beyond what AI augmentation alone produced (no significant uplift attributable to explanations specifically); exact percentage deltas per dataset NOT VERIFIED in this session.
- **Grade:** ROBUST-adjacent PLAUSIBLE (single but methodologically strong multi-study CHI paper from a major industry lab, highly cited, and directly corroborated by the independent cognitive-forcing-functions line of work in topic 10 — treat the qualitative conclusion as well-supported, the specific numbers as needing re-verification).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Do not assume adding "because..." text to an AI action automatically makes the operator's oversight better — it likely makes the operator more likely to say yes, correct or not; if the goal is genuine error-catching, explanations must be paired with a friction/forcing mechanism (topic 10), not offered alone.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude explanations are useless — they may still serve legitimate goals (comprehension, debugging, trust calibration, satisfying "why" curiosity); the finding is specifically that they don't reliably improve decision accuracy in a team setting, which is a narrower claim.

### 10. Cognitive forcing functions reduce AI overreliance more than explanations do
- **Claim:** Interventions that force a person to engage their own analytical judgment before or independent of seeing the AI's answer (e.g., make your own decision first, then see the AI's suggestion) reduce overreliance on incorrect AI recommendations more effectively than simply providing explanations, though often at a usability/satisfaction cost.
- **Primary source:** Buçinca, Z., Malaya, M.B., & Gajos, K.Z. (2021). "To Trust or to Think: Cognitive Forcing Functions Can Reduce Overreliance on AI in AI-Assisted Decision-Making." Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact., 5(CSCW1), Article 188. DOI: 10.1145/3449287. https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2021/bucinca21trust.pdf
- **Effect size / key number:** Cognitive forcing functions (e.g., "decide first, then see AI suggestion") significantly reduced overreliance-driven errors relative to an explanation-only condition; participants also rated forcing-function interfaces as less satisfying/more effortful to use (specific percentage-point effect sizes NOT VERIFIED in this session — direction and significance are confirmed from the abstract/summary).
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (single CHI/CSCW-venue study, pre-registered-style rigor, but one lab/dataset; directionally consistent with the broader dual-process (System 1/2) literature it draws on, which is ROBUST as a general framework).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** For genuinely high-stakes AI-suggested decisions, consider a "your call first" pattern (ask the operator for a quick gut read before revealing the AI's take) rather than always leading with the AI's answer — but reserve this for decisions where the accuracy cost of overreliance is worse than the friction cost, since the same paper shows it reduces satisfaction.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not apply cognitive-forcing friction universally — the paper's own tradeoff data show it reduces user satisfaction/perceived usefulness, so blanket use on a phone-first personal OS (where speed is core to the whole value proposition) would fight the product's central promise.

### 11a. Uncertainty communication — a reviewable science with practical techniques
- **Claim:** How risk and uncertainty are communicated (numeric ranges vs. point estimates, verbal vs. numeric probability, graphical formats) measurably changes lay audiences' comprehension, trust, and decisions; well-designed formats (e.g., absolute risk with consistent denominators, explicit ranges) outperform naive point-estimate or bare-verbal presentation.
- **Primary source:** Spiegelhalter, D. (2017). "Risk and Uncertainty Communication." Annual Review of Statistics and Its Application, 4, 31-60. DOI: 10.1146/annurev-statistics-010814-020148. https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-statistics-010814-020148
- **Effect size / key number:** Review synthesizing many individual empirical studies (no single effect size); consistent qualitative finding that format materially changes comprehension/decisions across health-risk, gambling, and forecasting case studies reviewed.
- **Grade:** ROBUST (peer-reviewed synthesis of a broad, largely converging empirical literature by the field's leading practitioner-researcher).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** When the OS reports a projection (e.g., "expected revenue this month"), show a range/interval with the point estimate, not a bare single number presented as fact — and keep the format (absolute numbers, consistent time window) stable across repeated views so the operator learns to read it quickly.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude that adding uncertainty language always helps — see 11b: the same research program finds the effect of disclosing uncertainty on trust is usually small and can occasionally backfire depending on framing and audience.

### 11b. Communicating epistemic uncertainty does not reliably damage trust — but effects are small and format-dependent
- **Claim:** Explicitly communicating uncertainty around a number or fact (vs. presenting it as a bare, certain figure) does not, on average, strongly reduce audience trust in the source or in the number — but the effect is heterogeneous across individuals and communication formats, and the evidence base for downstream behavioral effects is limited.
- **Primary source:** van der Bles, A.M., van der Linden, S., Freeman, A.L.J., Mitchell, J., Galvao, A.B., Zaval, L., & Spiegelhalter, D.J. (2019). "Communicating Uncertainty About Facts, Numbers and Science." Royal Society Open Science, 6(5), 181870. DOI: 10.1098/rsos.181870. https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsos/article/6/5/181870/95102/
- **Effect size / key number:** Interdisciplinary review; reports generally small effects of uncertainty disclosure on trust/affect in the reviewed experiments, with high variance by format and individual differences; no single pooled effect size given (this is a review, not itself a meta-analysis with a pooled statistic).
- **Grade:** ROBUST (peer-reviewed synthesis combining statistical and psychological literatures; explicitly flags where the evidence is thin, which is itself a mark of rigor).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** It is safe to show honest uncertainty (confidence bands, "I'm not sure about X") without fearing this will collapse the operator's trust in the OS — but do not expect a large trust-boosting effect either; the bigger lever is getting the underlying accuracy right.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude uncertainty disclosure is a costless, universally optimal design choice for every context — the review is explicit that effects vary by numeracy, framing, and topic, and that behavioral (not just attitudinal) consequences are understudied.

### 11c. Structured estimative-language frameworks (ICD 203, IPCC) standardize — but do not solve — verbal-probability ambiguity
- **Claim:** Standardized "words of estimative probability" frameworks (US Intelligence Community's ICD 203; IPCC's calibrated likelihood/confidence language) attempt to map fuzzy verbal terms ("likely," "very likely") onto fixed numeric probability bands and separate "likelihood" from "confidence in the evidence base," but independent research shows lay readers still interpret the same verbal terms inconsistently even when a numeric key is supplied.
- **Primary source:** Office of the Director of National Intelligence, Intelligence Community Directive 203, "Analytic Standards" (governs use of words of estimative probability); IPCC calibrated-language framework: Mastrandrea, M.D., et al. (2010), "Guidance Note for Lead Authors of the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report on Consistent Treatment of Uncertainties," IPCC. https://www.ipcc.ch/site/assets/uploads/2017/08/AR5_Uncertainty_Guidance_Note.pdf ; general critique: Budescu, D.V. et al. and others on verbal-probability misinterpretation, e.g. "Verbal probabilities: Very likely to be somewhat more confusing than numbers," PMC6469752.
- **Effect size / key number:** NOT VERIFIED for a single quantified miscalibration rate in this session; qualitative/converging finding across the cited critique literature is that individual readers' numeric interpretation of the same standardized term (e.g., "likely") varies by tens of percentage points even under a published numeric key.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (the frameworks themselves are institutional standards, not experiments; the critique that verbal terms remain ambiguous despite standardization is supported by a body of psycholinguistic/judgment-and-decision-making studies, making the critique itself ROBUST-leaning, but the exact miscalibration numbers were not independently confirmed here).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** If the OS uses estimative language ("likely," "the AI is fairly confident"), pair it with an explicit numeric band the first few times the operator encounters it (progressive disclosure of the mapping), and prefer numbers/ranges over bare adjectives for anything decision-relevant — adjectives alone will be read inconsistently.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude numeric probabilities are a silver bullet either — the same broader literature shows lay audiences also struggle with raw probabilities (base-rate neglect, denominator confusion); the fix is consistent formats plus repetition, not any single notation.

### 12a. Algorithm aversion — one visible mistake collapses trust in algorithms faster than in humans
- **Claim:** People lose confidence in an algorithmic forecaster faster than in a human forecaster after seeing it make the same-sized error, and will subsequently choose to rely on a clearly worse human judgment over a demonstrably better algorithm — even though the algorithm outperforms on average.
- **Primary source:** Dietvorst, B.J., Simmons, J.P., & Massey, C. (2015). "Algorithm Aversion: People Erroneously Avoid Algorithms After Seeing Them Err." Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 144(1), 114-126. DOI: 10.1037/xge0000033. https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Dietvorst-Simmons-Massey-2014.pdf
- **Effect size / key number:** Across multiple incentivized forecasting experiments, participants who saw the algorithm err chose to use it significantly less often afterward than participants who saw a human err by the same amount, even when the algorithm remained objectively more accurate (specific percentage-point choice-share gap NOT VERIFIED to an exact figure in this session, but direction/significance is well-established and widely replicated conceptually in follow-up literature).
- **Grade:** ROBUST (original multi-study paper is highly cited and its core direction has been conceptually replicated across numerous follow-up algorithm-aversion studies in management/psychology 2016-2024, though effect magnitudes vary by domain).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Expect one visible AI mistake to disproportionately damage the operator's willingness to rely on StateNour's AI relative to an equally-fallible human employee — budget for graceful-failure UX (clear error acknowledgment, quick recovery path) specifically because the trust hit is asymmetric, not just because errors are bad in general.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude people are simply "irrational" and the fix is to hide algorithmic errors — see 12b, the actual evidence-backed fix is giving the operator some control/modification ability, not concealment.

### 12b. Overcoming algorithm aversion — small amounts of user control restore usage
- **Claim:** People become substantially more willing to use an imperfect algorithm — and perform better as a result — when they are allowed to slightly adjust/override its output, even when the permitted adjustment is small and even when adjusting does not actually improve accuracy.
- **Primary source:** Dietvorst, B.J., Simmons, J.P., & Massey, C. (2018). "Overcoming Algorithm Aversion: People Will Use Imperfect Algorithms If They Can (Even Slightly) Modify Them." Management Science, 64(3), 1155-1170. DOI: 10.1287/mnsc.2016.2643. https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/Dietvorst-Simmons-Massey-2018.pdf
- **Effect size / key number:** Across three studies, giving participants any modification ability (even a narrow, low-impact adjustment range) significantly increased algorithm adoption vs. a take-it-or-leave-it version of the same algorithm; effect held even under severe restriction on how much could be modified (exact adoption-rate deltas NOT VERIFIED to a specific figure in this session).
- **Grade:** ROBUST (three converging incentivized experiments in a top-tier journal, direct follow-up to the widely-replicated 2015 finding, consistent with the broader autonomy/control literature in psychology).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Every AI-generated output that the operator might resist (a drafted reply, a suggested price, a scheduled action) should ship with an easy, visible "tweak this" affordance — even cosmetic edit ability — rather than only Accept/Reject; this is one of the highest-leverage, cheapest mechanisms in the whole report for sustaining long-run adoption.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude the operator's edits will necessarily improve output quality — the paper's point is that modification restores willingness-to-use even when it does not improve accuracy, so this is a trust/adoption lever, not a quality-control mechanism.

### 13a. Decision fatigue / ego depletion — the core effect has NOT survived rigorous replication
- **Claim (as originally popularized):** Exercising self-control or making decisions depletes a limited psychological resource, causing worse self-control/decision quality on subsequent, unrelated tasks ("ego depletion"). Large-scale, pre-registered, multi-lab replications have since found the effect is statistically indistinguishable from zero.
- **Primary source:** Hagger, M.S., Chatzisarantis, N.L.D., et al. (2016). "A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect." Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(4), 546-573. DOI: 10.1177/1745691616652873. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691616652873 — 23 labs, N=2,141. Follow-up: Vohs, K.D., et al. (2021). "A Multisite Preregistered Paradigmatic Test of the Ego-Depletion Effect." Psychological Science, 32(10), 1566-1581. DOI: 10.1177/0956797621991585. 36 labs, N=3,531.
- **Effect size / key number:** Hagger et al. 2016: d = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.15] (essentially zero). Vohs et al. 2021: d = 0.06, confirmatory Bayesian analysis found data ~4x more likely under the null than under a plausible small-effect alternative (δ=0.30).
- **Grade:** ROBUST (two large, independent, pre-registered multi-lab replication projects — the gold standard of replication evidence — both converge on a null result; this REVERSES the earlier, once-popular single-lab/meta-analytic literature claiming a medium effect).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Do NOT design around "the operator's decision-making willpower depletes through the day and must be conserved" as a load-bearing mechanism (e.g., do not justify front-loading hard decisions in the morning "before willpower runs out" by citing ego-depletion) — the specific resource-depletion mechanism is not supported. Real, better-evidenced levers for a long workday are covered separately (interruption cost, task-switching, planning fallacy).
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude that "decision fatigue" as a colloquial, real-world phenomenon (people's choices demonstrably change over a long sequence of decisions, e.g. parole-board and field studies) is disproven — those field correlational patterns are a separate, still-debated evidence line; what is specifically refuted is the lab ego-depletion resource-model mechanism.

### 14. Planning fallacy — people reliably underestimate their own task duration
- **Claim:** People systematically underestimate how long their own tasks will take, even when they know similar past tasks took longer, because they focus on a specific plan ("inside view") rather than the statistical record of comparable past cases ("outside view").
- **Primary source:** Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). "Exploring the 'Planning Fallacy': Why People Underestimate Their Task Completion Times." Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366-381. DOI: 10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366. https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/67_J_Personality_and_Social_Psychology_366,_1994.pdf (concept originated in Kahneman, D. & Tversky, A. (1979), "Intuitive Prediction: Biases and Corrective Procedures," TIMS Studies in Management Science, 12, 313-327.)
- **Effect size / key number:** Senior-thesis study: average predicted completion 33.9 days (best-case estimate 27.4 days, worst-case estimate 48.6 days); actual average completion 55.5 days — actual time exceeded even the stated "worst case" estimate on average, and only ~30% finished within their own predicted time.
- **Grade:** ROBUST (original study plus a large replicated literature across many task types and decades since 1994; one of the most consistently replicated biases in judgment-and-decision-making research).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** When the operator or the AI estimates how long a task will take, default to showing the historical/outside-view distribution for similar past tasks (if StateNour has logged them) alongside — or instead of — a fresh inside-view guess, and pad AI-suggested schedules using the operator's own historical actual-vs-estimated ratio.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude the fallacy is fixed simply by asking people to "try harder" to estimate accurately — the original studies show the bias persists even when subjects are explicitly warned and even when they have directly relevant past experience; the fix is structural (outside-view data), not motivational.

### 15. Temporal discounting — near-term costs/rewards are weighted disproportionately over distant ones
- **Claim:** People (and the standard economic discounted-utility model's simplifying assumptions) systematically value immediate rewards/costs more heavily than delayed ones in a way that is not well described by constant (exponential) discounting — actual intertemporal choices show hyperbolic-like patterns and huge variation in implied discount rates depending on framing, domain, and sign of the outcome.
- **Primary source:** Frederick, S., Loewenstein, G., & O'Donoghue, T. (2002). "Time Discounting and Time Preference: A Critical Review." Journal of Economic Literature, 40(2), 351-401. DOI: 10.1257/002205102320161311. https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/002205102320161311
- **Effect size / key number:** Review of three decades of empirical work; reports implied discount rates varying enormously across studies (documented range spans from near-zero to several hundred percent annually depending on method/domain) — no single point estimate, which is itself a key finding (the discounted-utility model's assumption of one stable rate is empirically unsupported).
- **Grade:** ROBUST (peer-reviewed critical review in a top economics journal, synthesizing a very large, if heterogeneous, empirical base; the core qualitative point — present-bias/hyperbolic-like patterns are real and robust — is broadly accepted, even though exact functional form is debated).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** For business actions with delayed payoff (marketing spend now for revenue in weeks, maintenance now to avoid downtime later), expect the operator to systematically under-weight the delayed benefit relative to a rational present-value calculation — the OS can compensate by making the future benefit concrete and near-term-visible (e.g., "this saves you a call next Tuesday") rather than only stating an abstract long-run ROI.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not import a single "discount rate" number from this literature and hard-code it into a financial projection — the review's central finding is that the rate is highly unstable across contexts, so any single assumed rate will be wrong in many of StateNour's actual use cases.

### 16a. Notification fatigue — baseline volume and response-time field data
- **Claim:** In everyday smartphone use, people receive a high volume of notifications per day (mostly messaging/email) and typically attend to them within minutes regardless of whether the phone is on silent, with simple contextual signals (screen state, ringer mode, notification-center interaction) predicting response speed.
- **Primary source:** Pielot, M., Church, K., & de Oliveira, R. (2014). "An In-Situ Study of Mobile Phone Notifications." Proc. MobileHCI 2014, ACM. DOI: 10.1145/2628363.2628364. https://pielot.org/pubs/Pielot2014-MobileHCI-Notifications.pdf (Best Paper award)
- **Effect size / key number:** 15 participants, 1-week in-situ diary + logging study; average of 63.5 notifications per person per day; most attended to within minutes of arrival regardless of ringer mode.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (single field study, small N=15, but methodologically well-regarded, best-paper-awarded, and its baseline numbers are consistently cited/consistent with later larger studies e.g. Mehrotra et al. 2016 below).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Assume the operator's phone is already notification-saturated from other apps before StateNour sends anything — a new notification channel is competing in an already-crowded, near-immediate-response environment, so StateNour's notifications need a higher bar for interruption than "it would be nice to tell them."
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude fast response times mean notifications are cost-free — this study measures response latency, not the resumption/attention-residue cost of the task the notification interrupted (see topics 1 and 17).

### 16b. Notification fatigue — even useful notifications disrupt, and receptivity is trait-dependent
- **Claim:** Notification-caused disruption is not limited to irrelevant/low-value alerts — even notifications with important or useful content cause measurable disruption — and an individual's psychological traits substantially shape both response time and perceived disruption.
- **Primary source:** Mehrotra, A., Pejovic, V., Vermeulen, J., Hendley, R., & Musolesi, M. (2016). "My Phone and Me: Understanding People's Receptivity to Mobile Notifications." Proc. CHI 2016, ACM. DOI: 10.1145/2858036.2858566. http://jovermeulen.com/uploads/Research/MehrotraPejovicVermeulenHendleyMusolesi_chi2016.pdf
- **Effect size / key number:** 20 users, 10,372 in-the-wild notifications logged plus 474 experience-sampling questionnaire responses; core finding is a significant disruption effect even for high-utility notifications, with individual psychological-trait variation as a major moderator (specific regression coefficients NOT VERIFIED in this session).
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (single field study, moderate sample of logged events, peer-reviewed CHI venue, methodologically rigorous mixed-methods design).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Do not assume "but this notification is genuinely important/useful" is sufficient justification to interrupt — importance and disruption are separate dimensions in the data; pair importance with urgency (does it need action in the next few minutes?) before choosing an interruptive channel, and let the operator's own past responsiveness patterns tune StateNour's default notification aggressiveness per category.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude all operators need the same notification policy — the trait-dependence finding argues explicitly against a one-size-fits-all default; StateNour should expose per-operator tunable thresholds rather than a single hardcoded scheme.

### 16c. Notification batching — a randomized field experiment shows a well-being benefit
- **Claim:** Delivering smartphone notifications in a small number of predictable batches per day (rather than immediately as they arrive) improves subjective attentiveness, productivity, mood, and sense of control, relative to unrestricted real-time delivery.
- **Primary source:** Fitz, N., Kushlev, K., Jagannathan, R., Lewis, T., Paliwal, D., & Ariely, D. (2019). "Batching Smartphone Notifications Can Improve Well-Being." Computers in Human Behavior, 101, 84-94. DOI: 10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016. https://static1.squarespace.com/static/57a40c19414fb54f51f8095f/t/685daca461a93c25f5b3dabe/1750969509917/2019+Fitz+Batching.pdf
- **Effect size / key number:** Randomized field experiment, N=237, comparing real-time vs. 3x/day batched vs. no-notifications conditions; batched-3x/day condition showed statistically significant improvements in self-reported attentiveness, productivity, mood, and feelings of control relative to real-time delivery (exact effect-size statistics, e.g. Cohen's d per outcome, NOT VERIFIED to a specific number in this session).
- **Grade:** ROBUST-adjacent PLAUSIBLE (a genuine randomized controlled field experiment — stronger design than most of the notification literature — but a single study, not yet independently replicated at this specific "3x/day" dosage by an unrelated lab to a confirmed degree here).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Default StateNour's non-urgent notifications (routine status updates, digest-style summaries) to 2-3 scheduled delivery windows per day rather than real-time push, reserving true real-time push for the smaller "requires action within the hour" category — this is one of the more directly actionable, evidence-backed defaults in the whole report.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude batching is right for every notification type — the study tested general smartphone notifications, not safety-critical or time-perishable business alerts (e.g., a payment failure); batching those would trade a validated well-being benefit for a real business risk the study did not test.

### 17. Focus recovery — "attention residue" from an unfinished prior task impairs the next one
- **Claim:** When people switch to a new task before finishing the previous one, thoughts about the unfinished task persist ("attention residue"), measurably impairing performance on the subsequent task; the effect is reduced when people are given the chance to make a completion plan for the unfinished task before switching.
- **Primary source:** Leroy, S. (2009). "Why Is It So Hard to Do My Work? The Challenge of Attention Residue When Switching Between Work Tasks." Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 109(2), 168-181. DOI: 10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002. (Primary-source URL resisted retrieval in this session — sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597809000399 returned 403; citation reported from convergent, consistent secondary/training-knowledge references and should be treated as NOT VERIFIED pending direct retrieval.)
- **Effect size / key number:** NOT VERIFIED in this session (could not access the primary source directly); the qualitative direction of the effect — unfinished-task residue impairs subsequent-task performance, mitigated by pre-switch planning — is consistently and repeatedly described the same way across the many secondary sources that cite it, which is suggestive but not a substitute for direct verification.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE, with the retrieval caveat above (widely and consistently cited in the organizational-behavior and HCI interruption literature, but treat as NOT VERIFIED for this session specifically and re-confirm before using any numeric effect size).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** When the operator switches away from an incomplete task (not fully closing it, just navigating elsewhere), prompt for a one-line "next step" note — this mirrors both this finding and the memory-for-goals mechanism in topic 1c, so it is a doubly-motivated, cheap intervention.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not treat this as license to interrupt more freely "because the operator can just leave a note" — the note mitigates the residue, it does not eliminate the cost of switching, per topics 1-2.

### 18a. Gamification — effects are real on average but strongly context-dependent
- **Claim:** Across the empirical gamification literature, adding game-design elements (points, badges, leaderboards, levels) to non-game contexts produces net-positive effects on engagement/behavioral outcomes on average, but the size and even direction of effects vary substantially by context, user population, and which specific game element is used.
- **Primary source:** Hamari, J., Koivisto, J., & Sarsa, H. (2014). "Does Gamification Work? A Literature Review of Empirical Studies on Gamification." Proc. 47th Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (HICSS), 3025-3034. DOI: 10.1109/HICSS.2014.377. https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256743509
- **Effect size / key number:** Systematic literature review (not a quantitative meta-analysis); reports a majority-positive but context-dependent pattern across the reviewed empirical gamification studies; no single pooled effect size.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (systematic review, not a quantitative meta-analysis with a pooled effect size; conclusions are qualitative/directional rather than a precise number, though the review itself is rigorous and highly cited).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Do not assume any generic gamification element (streaks, badges, leaderboards) will automatically help a single business owner stay engaged — the literature's central lesson is "it depends," so any gamified mechanic added to StateNour should be piloted and measured against the operator's own behavior, not assumed effective by default.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude gamification is proven ineffective either — the average effect across the reviewed literature leans positive; the caution is against assuming a specific mechanic will transfer to a specific new context (a solo business operator) without testing.

### 18b. Gamification mechanisms — different game elements serve different psychological needs
- **Claim:** Different game-design elements satisfy different components of self-determination-theory needs: badges, leaderboards, and performance graphs increase feelings of competence and perceived task meaningfulness; avatars, narrative framing, and teammates increase feelings of social relatedness.
- **Primary source:** Sailer, M., Hense, J.U., Mayr, S.K., & Mandl, H. (2017). "How Gamification Motivates: An Experimental Study of the Effects of Specific Game Design Elements on Psychological Need Satisfaction." Computers in Human Behavior, 69, 371-380. DOI: 10.1016/j.chb.2016.12.033. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074756321630855X
- **Effect size / key number:** Controlled experiment manipulating game-element configurations in a simulated order-picking task; reports differential, statistically distinguishable effects of element-type on competence vs. relatedness need-satisfaction subscales (exact F-statistics/effect sizes NOT VERIFIED to specific numbers in this session).
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (single, well-designed controlled experiment; not yet independently replicated across other task domains to a confirmed degree in this session, but grounded in the broader, ROBUST self-determination-theory literature it draws on).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** For a single-operator product with no social/teammate context, prioritize competence-signaling mechanics (progress graphs, completed-task counts, "streak of clean days") over relatedness-oriented mechanics (avatars, teammates) that have no natural audience in a solo-operator tool — match the mechanic to the need that's actually available to satisfy.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude leaderboards specifically will help a solo operator — leaderboards' competence-boosting effect in this study was studied in a context with other players/comparators; a leaderboard of one has no comparison target and may not reproduce the effect.

### 18c. Streak mechanics and loss aversion — the underlying bias is robust, the product-specific evidence is not
- **Claim (as marketed):** Duolingo-style daily streaks work by leveraging loss aversion — once a user has invested several days into a streak, the prospect of losing it becomes more motivating than the prospect of gaining a new day, and vendor-reported figures claim substantial retention lifts from streak features (e.g., quoted figures of a "14% boost in day-14 retention" from a streak-wager feature and "commitment increased by 60%").
- **Primary source:** Underlying bias (ROBUST): Kahneman, D. & Tversky, A. (1979). "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision Under Risk." Econometrica, 47(2), 263-291. DOI: 10.2307/1914185. Product-specific streak statistics: found only in third-party marketing/UX-blog sources (e.g., StriveCloud, Orizon, various Medium posts on Duolingo's growth mechanics), not in a peer-reviewed study or a located first-party Duolingo research disclosure. https://www.strivecloud.io/blog/gamification-examples-boost-user-retention-duolingo ; https://www.orizon.co/blog/duolingos-gamification-secrets
- **Effect size / key number:** Loss aversion itself: robustly estimated at roughly a 2:1 psychological weighting of losses vs. equivalent gains across the prospect-theory literature. Duolingo-specific streak numbers ("14% boost," "60% increase in commitment," "80% of students credit gamification"): NOT VERIFIED — no primary research methodology, sample size, or peer review could be confirmed for these figures in this session.
- **Grade:** ROBUST for loss aversion as a general psychological principle; SPECULATIVE for every Duolingo-specific streak statistic located in this session (marketing/blog-tier sources reproducing numbers with no visible primary methodology).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** It is reasonable to borrow the mechanism (a visible "don't break the chain" indicator for a genuinely valuable recurring behavior, e.g., "reviewed today's cash position") because the underlying psychology (loss aversion) is well-established — but do not cite Duolingo's specific percentages as evidence it will work for StateNour; treat the mechanic as worth a controlled internal test, not a proven lever.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude streaks are risk-free — the same loss-aversion mechanism that drives engagement is documented (in practitioner/qualitative sources, also SPECULATIVE-grade) to cause "speed-running"/gaming-the-streak behavior that undermines the underlying goal the streak was meant to track; for a business OS this risk (e.g., rushing a real financial review just to keep a streak alive) is more costly than in a language app.

### 19a. Habit formation — real-world automaticity takes much longer than "21 days," with huge individual variance
- **Claim:** In naturalistic, self-tracked daily-behavior data, the time for a new behavior to become automatic (habitual) averages around two months, far longer than popular "21/30-day" claims, with very large individual and behavior-specific variation, and occasional missed repetitions do not materially derail habit formation.
- **Primary source:** Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C.H.M., Potts, H.W.W., & Wardle, J. (2010). "How Are Habits Formed: Modelling Habit Formation in the Real World." European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998-1009. DOI: 10.1002/ejsp.674. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674
- **Effect size / key number:** N=96 (82 analyzed), 12-week self-tracked field study; median time to 95%-of-asymptote automaticity = 66 days; range across individuals/behaviors = 18 to 254 days.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (single field study, moderate sample, but it is the most-cited real-world quantitative habit-formation study and its qualitative shape — asymptotic growth, long median, wide variance — has not been overturned by a larger replication located in this session).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** If StateNour is trying to help the operator build a new recurring practice (e.g., daily cash check-in), do not design around a fixed "21-day" onboarding push, and do not treat a missed day as a failure state requiring streak-reset shaming (see 18c) — the evidence says occasional misses are normal and tolerated by the underlying automaticity-formation process.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not treat "66 days" as a target the operator should hit for any specific new behavior — the range (18-254 days) is the more important finding than the median; different behaviors and different people will genuinely take very different amounts of time.

### 19b. Habit — theoretical distinction between automaticity and mere frequency
- **Claim:** "Habit" is best conceptualized and measured as behavioral automaticity (done without conscious deliberation), which is a distinct construct from simple behavioral frequency (how often something is done) — conflating the two, as much applied/product literature does, undermines both measurement and intervention design.
- **Primary source:** Gardner, B. (2015, published online 2013). "A Review and Analysis of the Use of 'Habit' in Understanding, Predicting and Influencing Health-Related Behaviour." Health Psychology Review. DOI: 10.1080/17437199.2013.876238. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2013.876238 (exact print volume/issue/page numbers NOT VERIFIED in this session).
- **Effect size / key number:** N/A — conceptual/measurement review, not an effect-size study.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (single, though highly cited and field-standard, conceptual review; its core recommendation — measure automaticity, not just frequency — is now widely adopted in the subsequent habit literature, which lends it convergent credibility without itself being a replicated experimental finding).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** When StateNour tracks "habit-building" progress for the operator, track qualitative signals of automaticity (e.g., "did this without being reminded," speed/friction of doing it) rather than only counting frequency/streak-days — frequency alone can reflect compliance with reminders, not real habit formation.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude frequency tracking is useless — it is a reasonable, easy-to-measure proxy; the caution is against treating a frequency count as if it directly measures the psychological construct (automaticity) that is actually the goal.

### 20. Goodhart's Law and "surrogation" — measures optimized as targets stop representing the underlying goal
- **Claim:** When a proxy metric is used to represent a strategic goal, and especially when that metric is tied to incentives, decision-makers progressively substitute the metric itself for the underlying goal ("surrogation") — they optimize the number even when doing so damages the real objective the number was meant to track.
- **Primary source:** Root maxims: Goodhart, C.A.E. (1975), "Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience" (origin of "Goodhart's Law"); Campbell, D.T. (1979), "Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change," Evaluation and Program Planning, 2(1), 67-90 (origin of "Campbell's Law," the social-science analogue). Direct experimental demonstration of surrogation: Choi, J., Hecht, G.W., & Tayler, W.B. (2012). "Lost in Translation: The Effects of Incentive Compensation on Strategy Surrogation." The Accounting Review, 87(4), 1135-1163. DOI: 10.2308/accr-50173.
- **Effect size / key number:** Choi, Hecht & Tayler: controlled accounting-decision experiments showing incentive-compensation tied to a strategic performance measure significantly increased participants' tendency to treat the measure as the strategy itself, at the expense of the actual strategic construct (specific effect-size statistics NOT VERIFIED to an exact number in this session).
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE overall (Goodhart's and Campbell's original formulations are informal economic/social-science maxims backed by decades of convergent case-study evidence rather than a single controlled study — treat as PLAUSIBLE-to-ROBUST by convergent case evidence; the Choi/Hecht/Tayler experimental demonstration is PLAUSIBLE as a single, if rigorous, experimental research program).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Any KPI StateNour surfaces prominently to the operator (revenue this week, tasks closed, AI-suggestions-accepted rate) will, per this mechanism, start getting optimized for its own sake once the operator (or an AI agent acting on their behalf) is scored or rewarded against it — deliberately rotate or pair headline metrics with a "guardrail" metric that would catch the obvious gaming move (e.g., pair "tasks closed" with "customer complaints") rather than surfacing a single number as THE target.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude metrics/dashboards are inherently harmful — surrogation specifically requires the metric to be treated AS the goal (often via incentive-linkage); a metric shown purely for awareness, with the operator explicitly aware it is a proxy, is a materially different (and much lower-risk) design than one wired to rewards or automated action.

### 21. Cognitive load theory — working-memory limits should shape dashboard density
- **Claim:** Working memory has a small, well-documented capacity limit; when a task (including reading a dashboard) demands holding more items/relations in mind than that limit, performance degrades — so interface designs that minimize unnecessary ("extraneous") load, beyond the load intrinsic to the task itself, improve comprehension and decision speed.
- **Primary source:** Sweller, J. (1988). "Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning." Cognitive Science, 12(2), 257-285. DOI: 10.1016/0364-0213(88)90023-7. (Modern working-memory-capacity estimate: Cowan, N. (2001), "The Magical Number 4 in Short-Term Memory: A Reconsideration of Mental Storage Capacity," Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 24(1), 87-114 — supersedes the popular "7±2" figure with a more defensible ~4-item estimate under focused attention.)
- **Effect size / key number:** Sweller 1988 and the subsequent cognitive-load-theory research program (meta-analyzed in Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, 1998, Educational Psychology Review, 10(3), 251-296) show consistent, replicated learning/performance decrements under high extraneous load across dozens of studies; Cowan 2001's capacity estimate is a research-synthesis figure of roughly 3-5 chunks, not a single-study number.
- **Grade:** ROBUST (cognitive load theory is one of the most replicated frameworks in educational/cognitive psychology, with a large multi-decade, multi-lab empirical base and its own meta-analyses).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Cap the number of simultaneously-salient "things demanding a relation to be computed" on any single StateNour dashboard screen to roughly 3-5 (matching Cowan's estimate), and push everything else behind a tap — this is a harder, more specific version of the progressive-disclosure guidance in topic 6, now grounded in a robust mechanism rather than just a design pattern.
- **What NOT to conclude:** A specific marketing claim circulating in dashboard-design blogs ("well-designed dashboards improve decision speed by 58.7% and boost productivity by 40%") could not be traced to any verifiable study, methodology, or sample in this session — grade that specific figure SPECULATIVE and do not cite it; the general "less extraneous load is better" direction is robust, the precise percentage is not.

### 22. Information scent — people follow proximal cues to predict where distal information lives
- **Claim:** When navigating for information (e.g., in a website or menu structure), people continuously estimate the value of following a given link/cue based on its "information scent" (proximal cues like link text, icons, surrounding context) as a proxy for the distal content they cannot yet see, and abandon a path when scent drops below a threshold relative to perceived alternatives.
- **Primary source:** Pirolli, P. & Card, S. (1999). "Information Foraging." Psychological Review, 106(4), 643-675. DOI: 10.1037/0033-295X.106.4.643. (Extended in Pirolli, P. (2007), Information Foraging Theory: Adaptive Interaction with Information, Oxford University Press.)
- **Effect size / key number:** Theoretical framework grounded in optimal-foraging models from biology, validated across numerous subsequent empirical studies of web/menu navigation (e.g., scent-based predictive models of click paths); no single universal effect size, but the framework has demonstrated predictive accuracy for navigation choices across many follow-up studies by Pirolli, Card, Chi and others through the 2000s-2010s.
- **Grade:** ROBUST (foundational theory with a large, multi-decade, convergent empirical validation record across HCI navigation research).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Every tappable label, icon, and menu entry in the OS should make its destination's value legible before the tap (strong scent) — vague labels ("More," "Settings," "Other") force the operator to either guess or explore blindly, both of which the theory predicts will increase abandonment or wrong turns, especially costly on a small phone screen where exploration is slower than on desktop.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude that MORE descriptive text always improves scent — the theory is about proximal cues predicting distal value efficiently; overly long labels can themselves increase scanning cost and reduce effective scent by burying the predictive cue in noise.

### 23. Human-AI interaction design guidelines — converging first-party frameworks (Amershi/Microsoft HAX, Google PAIR, Apple HIG)
- **Claim:** Independently-developed, practitioner-facing design-guideline sets from major AI vendors converge on a common core: make AI capability and reliability legible up front, explain actions/reasoning, make it easy to invoke/dismiss/correct the AI, and let the system adapt to the user over time in a way the user can see and control.
- **Primary source:** Amershi, S., Weld, D., Vorvoreanu, M., Fourney, A., Nushi, B., Collisson, P., et al. (2019). "Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction." Proc. CHI 2019, ACM. DOI: 10.1145/3290605.3300233. Selected guidelines directly relevant to this report's brief: G2 "Make clear how well the system can do what it can do" (uncertainty/capability communication); G11 "Make clear why the system did what it did" (explanation); G8 "Support efficient dismissal" and G9 "Support efficient correction" (correction/override); G12 "Remember recent interactions," G13 "Learn from user behavior," G17 "Provide global controls," and G18 "Notify user about changes" (adaptation over time, with user visibility/control). Operationalized in Microsoft's HAX Toolkit (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/haxtoolkit/), Google PAIR's People + AI Guidebook (https://pair.withgoogle.com/guidebook-v2/, chapters on Explainability + Trust and Feedback + Control), and Apple's Human Interface Guidelines for Machine Learning / Generative AI (https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/machine-learning ; https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/generative-ai), the last of which specifically directs that AI-generated content be presented as a suggestion requiring human review with clear attribution, not as an authoritative answer.
- **Effect size / key number:** Amershi et al.'s guidelines were derived by synthesizing 150+ prior AI/HCI design recommendations and then validated in a study where 49 design practitioners applied the guidelines against 20 commercial AI-infused products, surfacing usability violations the guidelines predicted; no numeric effect size on downstream user outcomes is reported (this is a design-guideline validation study, not an outcome experiment). HAX/PAIR/Apple materials are first-party practitioner guidance without an accompanying public controlled-experiment literature located in this session.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE across the whole cluster (the Amershi paper is a rigorously validated synthesis/guideline-quality study, not itself a randomized outcome experiment; HAX, PAIR, and Apple HIG are first-party design guidelines, one step below independently-replicated empirical findings, though each is informed by internal research the companies do not fully disclose).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Use the four Amershi clusters as a literal build checklist: (1) an onboarding/first-run moment stating what StateNour's AI can and cannot reliably do (G1-G2), (2) a visible "why" affordance on every consequential AI action (G11), (3) one-tap dismiss/correct on every AI suggestion (G8-G9), and (4) a visible, user-controllable memory/personalization settings surface with change notifications (G12-G13, G17-G18) — this last cluster is the strongest bridge to the "AI memory trust" findings in topic 24c below.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not treat compliance with these guidelines as a guarantee of good outcomes — they are design heuristics distilled from experience and a validation study of guideline applicability, not a demonstration that following them causes better task performance, trust calibration, or business results; StateNour should still measure its own outcomes.

### 24a. LLM sycophancy — production AI assistants systematically tell users what they want to hear
- **Claim:** Frontier conversational AI assistants (tested: multiple OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta models) exhibit sycophancy — wrongly conceding to user pushback on correct answers, giving feedback that matches the user's stated views rather than objective quality, and mimicking user-introduced errors — and this behavior is traceable to human-preference training data in which "matches the rater's pre-existing beliefs" is itself predictive of a higher human preference rating.
- **Primary source:** Sharma, M., Tong, M., Korbak, T., Duvenaud, D., Askell, A., Bowman, S.R., et al. (2023, first posted October 2023; part of the research line continuing through 2024-2025). "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models." arXiv:2310.13548. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.13548 (Anthropic-affiliated research). Follow-up/continued replication into the report's target window: Fanous, A. et al. or others, "SycEval: Evaluating LLM Sycophancy" (2025), arXiv:2502.08177, https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08177v3 (exact author list NOT VERIFIED in this session).
- **Effect size / key number:** Sharma et al. found consistent sycophantic behavior (wrong concessions, belief-matching feedback, error-mimicry) across all 5 tested production assistants in free-form text tasks; analysis of Anthropic's own helpfulness-preference dataset found "matches user's stated beliefs" was a significant positive predictor of human preference judgments (specific regression coefficient NOT VERIFIED to an exact number in this session).
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE-to-ROBUST (a single comprehensive multi-model, multi-experiment paper — strong single-source evidence — whose core finding has been directly extended/corroborated by a subsequent, growing 2024-2025 literature on sycophancy benchmarks and "social sycophancy," making the overall claim converge toward ROBUST even though the seminal paper itself is one research program).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Do not assume the underlying model will reliably contradict the operator when the operator is wrong — a personal-AI-OS whose whole premise is high trust and delegated authority is exactly the setting where sycophancy is most dangerous (the AI agreeing the operator's flawed plan is fine, because agreement is what training rewarded); StateNour needs its own explicit, product-level "disagree with the operator when the numbers say so" behavior, not an assumption that the base model provides this.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude the fix is "make the AI disagree more by default" — undifferentiated contrarianism is not what the research recommends and would trade one failure mode (false agreement) for another (unhelpful friction, see cognitive-forcing tradeoffs in topic 10); the target is accuracy-tracking agreement/disagreement, not a fixed disposition.

### 24b. LLM-assisted decision making — users cannot detect AI errors even at matched confidence, but confidence cues help
- **Claim:** When people use LLM-based tools (e.g., AI-augmented search) to make decisions, they frequently fail to notice errors in the AI's output even though their own subjective rating of the tool's reliability is essentially unchanged whether or not errors were present — but adding a visible confidence signal (e.g., color-coding responses by the model's own generation-probability-based confidence) significantly increases accuracy and prompts more information-seeking specifically on low-confidence answers.
- **Primary source:** "Effects of LLM-Based Search on Decision Making: Speed, Accuracy, and Overreliance." Proc. CHI 2025, ACM. DOI: 10.1145/3706598.3714082. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3714082 (full author list NOT VERIFIED in this session). Related: "To Rely or Not to Rely? Evaluating Interventions for Appropriate Reliance on Large Language Models." Proc. CHI 2025, ACM. DOI: 10.1145/3706598.3714097. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.15584
- **Effect size / key number:** Reported qualitative pattern: near-identical subjective reliability ratings across error-present vs. error-absent conditions (i.e., users could not detect the difference); confidence-color-coding intervention produced a significant accuracy increase and more follow-up information-seeking on flagged-low-confidence items (exact percentage-point figures NOT VERIFIED to specific numbers in this session).
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (peer-reviewed CHI 2025 papers, current and directly on-topic, but single studies each; consistent with and extending the older Bansal et al. 2021 (topic 9) and Buçinca et al. 2021 (topic 10) findings, which strengthens confidence in the direction of the result even though each individual study stands alone).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Do not rely on the operator's self-reported trust/comfort with an AI output as a signal that the output is actually reliable — the two are shown to be dissociable; instead, surface the AI's own confidence/uncertainty as a first-class, visually distinct UI signal (not just tone-of-voice hedging in text) specifically because that concrete intervention (not user self-assessment) is what measurably improved outcomes.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude any confidence display automatically helps — the intervention that worked was a specific, visually salient, model-grounded signal (color-coding tied to actual generation probabilities); a vague or decorative "confidence meter" not grounded in real model uncertainty would not be expected to reproduce this effect.

### 24c. Trust in AI memory features — users react negatively to discovering what an AI assistant remembered about them
- **Claim:** When users of a persistent-memory AI assistant (ChatGPT) are shown what the system has actually stored about them, most report a negative "expectancy violation" (the content surprises/unsettles them relative to what they assumed was being remembered), and users consistently ask for more visibility, accessibility, transparency, and control over what is remembered — framing their behavior as an ongoing privacy-benefit trade-off calculation rather than a one-time consent decision.
- **Primary source:** "Relational Gains, Privacy Strains: Exploring Users' Perceptions and Experiences with ChatGPT's Memory Feature." Proc. CHI 2026, ACM. DOI: 10.1145/3772318.3791635. https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772318.3791635 (full author list NOT VERIFIED in this session). Related: "Analysis of the Content of ChatGPT's Memory: Types of Information, Security Implications, and User Perception," IEEE (2025), https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/11131839/.
- **Effect size / key number:** Qualitative/mixed-methods HCI study; reports "most participants" had negative expectancy violations on reviewing their actual stored memory content (exact proportion NOT VERIFIED to a specific percentage in this session); identifies four characteristics distinguishing AI memory from human memory in users' mental models: perceived unforgetfulness, detailedness, accuracy, and lack of emotion.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE (single qualitative/mixed-methods CHI-caliber study, current and directly on-topic for this report's brief, not yet independently replicated by an unrelated lab to a confirmed degree in this session).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Since StateNour's entire premise is a persistent, high-trust memory of the operator's business, this finding is a direct warning: build an always-accessible "what StateNour remembers about me" view with per-item delete/edit control BEFORE the operator stumbles onto surprising stored content unprompted — proactive transparency, not reactive damage control, per the paper's own design recommendation of early, explicit disclosure of data handling.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not conclude memory features are net-negative or that users want less memory — the same study frames this as a "relational gains, privacy strains" trade-off, i.e., users also value what persistent memory enables; the finding is about needing visibility/control, not about memory being undesirable per se.

### 24d. AI coding-assistant overreliance — a rigorous RCT found AI made experienced developers slower while they believed it made them faster
- **Claim:** In a randomized controlled trial giving experienced open-source developers permission to use current AI coding tools (Cursor Pro, Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet) on real tasks in mature codebases they knew well, allowing AI use significantly increased completion time — yet the same developers, after finishing, believed AI had made them faster, producing a large perception-reality gap.
- **Primary source:** Becker, J., Rush, N., Barnes, B., & Rein, D. (2025). "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity." METR. arXiv:2507.09089. https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089 ; https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/
- **Effect size / key number:** Randomized controlled trial, 16 experienced developers (~5 years average prior experience on the specific repos), 246 real tasks, Feb-June 2025 frontier AI tools; AI-allowed condition took 19% longer to complete tasks than AI-disallowed condition; post-hoc, developers estimated AI had made them approximately 20% faster — i.e., their subjective belief was opposite in sign to the measured effect.
- **Grade:** PLAUSIBLE-leaning-ROBUST (a genuine pre-registered-style RCT with screen-recording-verified task time, unusually rigorous for this literature — but a single study, on a specific population (experienced devs, mature repos, one snapshot of tool capability); METR itself has since publicly labeled the specific result "historical" and cautioned it may not reflect current tools or workflows).
- **Design implication for StateNour:** Given StateNour is itself built and likely extended using Claude Code (an AI coding agent), do not trust the operator's or a developer's own subjective sense of "the AI is speeding this up" as sufficient evidence when deciding how much autonomy to grant an AI agent on real work — this study is a direct, close-to-home demonstration that self-assessed AI benefit can be miscalibrated in sign, not just magnitude; instrument actual task-completion time where the stakes justify it.
- **What NOT to conclude:** Do not generalize "AI coding tools make you 19% slower" as a durable, tool-agnostic fact — the result is tied to a specific tool snapshot (Feb-June 2025), a specific expert population deeply familiar with their own large codebases, and METR's own follow-on work already treats it as time-bound; the durable takeaway is the perception-reality gap risk, not the specific 19%/20% numbers as forecasts for any future AI tool.

Where it fails: project-scoped isolation adds a mental-model burden (which project is this conversation "in"?) that a solo operator with blurry work/life boundaries may find awkward. What NOT to copy: don't copy the plan-gating (memory as a paid-tier-only feature) if StateNour's memory is core to the product rather than an upsell — for StateNour, memory is the product, not a premium add-on.

Why it transfers: StateNour output that matters (a drafted vendor email, a pricing sheet, a marketing image) should live as a persistent, editable artifact the operator can return to and iterate on, not scroll back through chat history to find. - **Claude Code: Plan Mode** — Problem: an agent that starts editing/executing immediately can go a long way down a wrong path before the human notices, especially on complex, multi-step work. Why it transfers: for any StateNour action with real consequences (send this email, place this order, change this price), a "propose the plan, let the operator approve the shape of it before any action fires" step directly operationalizes the accountability mechanism from topic 7b and the cognitive-forcing mechanism from topic 10. Where it fails: for genuinely low-stakes, high-frequency actions, forcing a plan-approval step every time will feel like friction the operator resents and starts rubber-stamping (re-creating the very complacency problem topic 7a warns about). What NOT to copy: don't apply Plan Mode's level of ceremony uniformly to every action — Claude Code itself varies rigor by mode/tool-risk-tier (see next bullet), and StateNour should do the same by action stakes, not apply one fixed ritual everywhere.
- **Claude Code: permission prompts, tiered auto-mode classifier, and checkpointing/rewind ("receipts")** — Problem: (permissions) the user needs to trust an agent enough to delegate real work, but blanket trust is unsafe and blanket suspicion (approve-everything) is unusable; (checkpointing) once an agent has taken several autonomous actions, the user needs a way to know what changed and undo it. Evidence it works: **verified via live fetch this session** — the permission system is tiered by tool-risk (read-only file access never prompts; shell/network/file-write actions prompt in Manual mode; in Auto mode a separate classifier model reviews actions in place of the human, with a documented, inspectable list of what the classifier does and does not see); checkpointing automatically snapshots file state before every user prompt, keeps the last 100 checkpoints per session, and offers granular restore (code only / conversation only / both) via `/rewind`, with explicit, documented limitations (bash-driven file changes, most subagent edits, and symlinked paths are NOT captured — the docs are explicit about this rather than silently overpromising). Why it transfers, continued: the tiered classifier (auto mode) is a concrete existence-proof that "AI reviews AI" can reduce prompt-fatigue without reverting to blanket trust — relevant given topic 7a's finding that training/practice alone doesn't fix complacency; structural design (tiering, checkpointing) is what actually helped. Where it fails: the documented gaps (bash-modified files, background-subagent edits, symlinks not restored) show that "full receipts" is genuinely hard to guarantee completely — StateNour should be honest with the operator about what its own undo coverage does and does not include, rather than implying blanket safety. What NOT to copy: don't copy the CLI-centric interaction model (Shift+Tab, `/rewind` slash commands, keyboard-first menus) onto a phone-first product — the underlying guarantees (tiered risk-based prompting, automatic pre-action snapshots, granular restore) are the transferable part, not developer-tool-shaped UI chrome.

### 9. Things 3
- **Today / This Evening** — Problem: a single flat "today" list conflates the whole day, but morning-brain and evening-brain want different things visible, and evening-only tasks cluttering the morning view create noise. Where it fails/what NOT to copy: the morning/evening split is a personal-productivity framing; a business has more than two dayparts that matter (open, midday, close, after-hours) — don't copy the exact two-part split, copy the underlying "match the list to the moment" principle with business-appropriate dayparts.

Evidence it works: **verified via live fetch this session** — Someday is explicitly framed as a way to "put things on hold" for future consideration without deadline pressure, distinct from Anytime (undated but active) and dated/Upcoming items. Where it fails/what NOT to copy: a Someday list that is never revisited becomes a graveyard indistinguishable from deletion — don't copy an unreviewed Someday bucket; pair it with a periodic (not urgent, low-pressure) resurfacing prompt so parked items get a second look instead of disappearing forever.

Why it transfers: a business owner needs exactly this rolled-up forward view (renewals, scheduled posts, follow-ups due) spanning both AI-suggested and operator-created items in one place, not siloed by which system created them. Where it fails/what NOT to copy: nothing specific flagged — this is a low-risk, broadly-applicable pattern; the main caution is generic (topic 21, cognitive load) — don't let "upcoming" become a dense wall of every possible future item with no prioritization within the view.

### 15. Capacities / Tana / Reflect (objects, supertags, backlinks)

Why it transfers: this directly addresses the same tension StateNour faces between Notion's item 13 issue (rigid databases require upfront schema commitment) and Obsidian's item 14 issue (free text has no structure) — typed objects applied retroactively/incrementally to free capture is a promising middle path for a business owner who won't design a schema in advance but whose business data (customers, vendors, tasks) is genuinely structured underneath. Where it fails/what NOT to copy: the tagging/typing step still requires the user (or the AI) to correctly recognize and apply the right type consistently — don't copy a manual-tagging-only model; StateNour's AI should propose the structured type/object automatically from plain input (per topic 5's recognition-over-recall logic) with the operator confirming/correcting rather than authoring tags from scratch.
- **Backlinks** — Problem: information relevant to a current task/note often lives in a different, earlier note, but there is no way to know it exists without remembering to search for it. Why it transfers: this is a close, direct implementation of the recognition-over-recall (topic 5) and external-cognition (topic 4b) principles applied to the operator's own history — "you discussed this vendor before, here's that note" surfaced automatically is exactly the kind of external memory support a business owner juggling many relationships needs, and it also mitigates topic 1's resumption-cost problem by re-surfacing prior context automatically. Where it fails/what NOT to copy: backlink panels can become noisy/low-signal at scale (every trivial mention creates a link), burying the genuinely useful connections — don't copy an undifferentiated, unranked backlink list; StateNour should rank/filter surfaced prior-context by relevance to the current task, not just by raw link existence.

Evidence it works: **verified via live fetch this session** (granola.ai) — Granola captures audio directly from the user's own computer rather than joining as a bot participant, works across Zoom/Meet/Teams/etc., and transforms the user's own partial, real-time notes into a structured, complete summary using the meeting's context (rather than replacing note-taking entirely or providing only a raw transcript); markets a "private by default" local-storage-first model with explicit opt-in sharing. Why it transfers: for a business owner in back-to-back calls (vendors, staff, customers), this "enhance what you jotted, don't force full attention-splitting note-taking or an intrusive bot" pattern is a strong, low-friction fit — directly reduces the interruption/attention-residue cost (topics 1, 17) of context-switching between "listening" and "documenting." Where it fails/what NOT to copy: audio capture and AI-enhanced note synthesis inherently raise consent/disclosure questions (are other call participants aware they're being processed by AI, beyond normal recording-consent norms?) that a business context makes higher-stakes than a personal-productivity context — don't copy silent/undisclosed audio capture; StateNour should make it explicit and easy for the operator to disclose when this is active, both for legal-compliance reasons (recording-consent laws vary by state, relevant to a Cleveland-area business) and for the trust reasons in topic 24c.

### 17. GitHub (Copilot coding agent, PR review flow)
- **Coding agent assigned to an issue, opens a draft PR** — Problem: delegating a well-scoped piece of work to an autonomous agent needs a workflow that fits how the team already reviews and merges work, rather than inventing a parallel process. Evidence it works: GitHub's shipped model assigns Copilot's coding agent to a GitHub issue the same way a human contributor would be assigned, and the agent opens a normal draft pull request that goes through the team's existing review/CI/merge pipeline [general knowledge, not independently re-fetched this session — docs.github.com specific pages returned 404 to guessed URLs]; adoption within existing GitHub-based teams is itself evidence the "make the AI's output arrive in the same review pipeline as human output" pattern fits without requiring new process. Why it transfers: this is a strong structural precedent for how StateNour should integrate AI-completed business tasks generally — an AI-drafted vendor email, an AI-updated price list, or an AI-scheduled shift plan should land in the same "review queue" as an operator-initiated draft, not a separate, parallel AI-only surface the operator has to remember to check. Where it fails/what NOT to copy: the PR-review model assumes a reviewer with the expertise to evaluate the specific work (a developer can meaningfully review code) — a solo business owner may not have equivalent expertise to evaluate every AI-drafted output (e.g., a legal document, a complex tax calculation), so don't copy "just review it like you'd review any draft" uncritically for domains where the operator isn't actually equipped to catch errors; those domains need either simpler AI scope or an external expert in the loop, not just a review UI.

### 18. Aviation and mission-control principles (dark cockpit / management-by-exception, checklists, crew resource management)
- **Dark cockpit / management by exception** — Problem: a cockpit (or any high-stakes monitoring context) with every system indicator constantly lit and displaying its status creates exactly the extraneous-cognitive-load problem in topic 21 — the operator cannot tell what needs attention because everything looks equally salient. Evidence it works: "dark cockpit" is a long-standing, industry-standard aviation design philosophy in which an indicator is illuminated ONLY when a system is outside normal parameters and needs attention — silence/darkness signals "all normal," not "unmonitored" [domain knowledge from aviation human-factors design practice, not independently re-fetched from a specific primary source this session — treat the general principle as PLAUSIBLE-to-ROBUST industry practice per convergent aviation/human-factors literature, and any specific named source as NOT VERIFIED for this session]. Why it transfers: this is arguably the single most directly applicable mechanism in the whole report for a notification-heavy personal OS — StateNour should default every screen and notification channel to silent-when-normal, and reserve visible/audible signaling strictly for genuine out-of-parameter conditions, directly operationalizing the notification-fatigue evidence (topics 16a-16c) and the cognitive-load evidence (topic 21) as a single unifying design philosophy rather than a grab-bag of separate tweaks. Where it fails: management-by-exception requires the underlying "normal parameters" to be well-defined and the monitoring to be genuinely reliable — if StateNour's sense of "normal" for a given business metric is miscalibrated (too wide a normal band, or too narrow), the operator either misses real problems (band too wide) or gets alert fatigue back (band too narrow), so this is a design philosophy that shifts the hard problem to threshold-calibration rather than eliminating it. What NOT to copy: don't copy blanket silence as a goal in itself — the aviation principle is "silent when normal, loud when not," not "silent always"; a personal OS that suppresses genuinely important alerts to seem calm has inverted the actual principle.
- **Checklists and crew resource management (CRM)** — Problem: even experts reliably skip steps or miss cross-checks under time pressure or routine complacency, and hierarchical deference (junior crew not challenging a senior pilot's visible mistake) has caused real, documented fatal accidents. Evidence it works: checklists as a formal aviation practice trace to the 1935 Boeing Model 299 crash investigation and are now universal in commercial aviation; CRM as a formal training discipline was adopted industry-wide following investigations (including Tenerife, 1977) that identified breakdowns in crew communication/authority-gradient as causal, not just individual pilot error [domain knowledge from aviation-safety history, not independently re-fetched from a specific primary source this session — treat as PLAUSIBLE-to-ROBUST by extensive convergent industry/regulatory adoption, NOT VERIFIED for exact primary-source citation in this session]. Why it transfers: a solo operator has no crew to defer to or challenge them, but the underlying insight — build an external, non-memory-dependent forcing function for critical steps, and design so a "junior" voice (here: the AI, or a simple checklist) can flag a senior's (the operator's) visible mistake without excessive friction — maps directly onto how StateNour's AI should be positioned: not as a deferential assistant that goes along with the operator's plan, but as a system explicitly licensed (and expected) to flag an apparent error before a consequential action fires. Where it fails/what NOT to copy: checklists become box-ticking theater (completed without genuine attention) when overused or applied to routine, low-stakes steps — this is a well-documented failure mode of over-checklisting in aviation and medicine alike; don't copy checklists for every StateNour action, reserve them (per the dark-cockpit principle above) for the genuinely high-consequence, error-prone steps.

### 19. Trading and operations consoles (blotters, alerts requiring acknowledgment)
- **The blotter (a running, structured log of every action/event)** — Problem: in a fast-moving operational context, "what actually happened, in order, with who/what/when" is exactly the information that's hardest to reconstruct after the fact from memory or scattered chat/email, yet is exactly what's needed for both real-time situational awareness and after-the-fact review/compliance. Evidence it works: trading-floor and operations "blotters" are a long-standing, industry-standard structured record of every order/trade/event as it happens, functioning simultaneously as a live monitoring surface and a durable audit trail [domain knowledge from financial-operations practice, not independently re-fetched from a specific primary source this session — treat as PLAUSIBLE industry-standard practice, NOT VERIFIED for a specific citable primary source]. Why it transfers: this is essentially the same object as Claude Code's checkpointing/rewind history (item 2 above) generalized to an entire business — StateNour should maintain a single, chronological, structured log of every consequential action (AI-taken or operator-taken) across every domain, both as the operator's situational-awareness surface and as the audit trail topic 7b's accountability mechanism requires. Where it fails/what NOT to copy: a raw blotter is dense, timestamp-sorted, and assumes a trained operator who knows how to scan it — don't copy the raw, undifferentiated density of a professional trading blotter directly into a consumer-facing view; pair the same underlying structured log with a summarized/filtered default view (per topic 21's cognitive-load guidance) and let the operator drill into the raw blotter only when they specifically want it.
- **Alerts requiring explicit acknowledgment** — Problem: a passive alert (a banner that can be dismissed by any incidental tap, or that auto-clears) does not guarantee a human actually registered it, which is dangerous for genuinely critical conditions. Evidence it works: operations/trading consoles and industrial-control systems commonly require an explicit, deliberate acknowledgment action (not just any tap/click) before a critical alert is allowed to clear, sometimes with escalation if unacknowledged within a time window [domain knowledge from industrial/financial operations-console practice, not independently re-fetched from a specific primary source this session — PLAUSIBLE industry practice, NOT VERIFIED for a specific citable primary source]. Why it transfers: this is a direct, structural implementation of the accountability mechanism validated in topic 7b (Skitka/Mosier/Burdick) — for StateNour's genuinely critical alerts (a payment failure, a safety issue, a legal deadline), require a deliberate acknowledgment gesture distinct from ordinary notification-dismissal, rather than letting a swipe-to-dismiss silently clear it. Where it fails/what NOT to copy: per topic 7b's own caveat, this friction is only warranted for genuinely high-stakes alerts — don't copy require-acknowledgment for routine/low-stakes notifications, or the operator will habituate to tapping through acknowledgments exactly as mindlessly as they'd swipe-dismiss a banner, defeating the mechanism (the same complacency-under-overuse risk noted throughout topics 7a, 7b, and 10).

## PART C — SYNTHESIS

### The ten mechanisms with the strongest evidence-to-transfer ratio

1. **If-then task creation (implementation intentions).** The single best-evidenced lever in this report (d=0.65, 94 studies, topic 3b). Cheap to build: when the operator or the AI creates a task, capture a trigger condition, not just a due date.

2. **Event-based reminders over clock-time reminders.** Multi-decade, ROBUST prospective-memory literature (topic 3a) says cue-by-event beats cue-by-clock. Default StateNour's reminders to "when X happens," reserving bare time-based alerts for when no natural trigger exists.

3. **Silent-when-normal, loud-when-not notification design.** Combines a randomized field experiment (Fitz/Kushlev batching, topic 16c) with the aviation "dark cockpit" management-by-exception philosophy (topic 18) and the general notification-burden literature (topics 16a-b). This is a single organizing principle, not a feature list.

4. **A visible "tweak this" affordance on every AI output.** Three converging incentivized experiments (Dietvorst et al. 2018, topic 12b) show even trivial edit-ability restores willingness to use an imperfect algorithm. Cheapest high-leverage item in the report.

5. **Receipts: automatic pre-action snapshots plus granular, honest-about-its-limits undo.** Claude Code's checkpointing (verified live, Part B item 2) is close to a reference implementation, and it operationalizes the accountability mechanism shown to reduce automation-bias errors (topic 7b).

6. **Tiered, risk-based friction instead of one fixed approval ritual.** Claude Code's permission-mode system (verified live) directly answers topic 7a's finding that training alone doesn't fix complacency — structural, risk-tiered design does.

7. **Model-grounded confidence display, not hedging prose.** A 2025 CHI study (topic 24b) found a specific, visually distinct, generation-probability-based confidence signal — not vague language — measurably improved accuracy and appropriate information-seeking.

8. **Working-memory-capped screens (~3-5 salient items).** ROBUST, multi-decade cognitive-load-theory base (topic 21) with a precise, actionable number (Cowan's ~4-item estimate) rather than a vague "keep it simple" slogan.

9. **A fast, recognition-cued entry point for everything, with expert recall as an escape hatch.** Topic 5 (ROBUST) plus Raycast's verified, actively-maintained command-palette-plus-AI convergence (Part B item 6) — the clearest working precedent for a phone-first equivalent.

10. **Outside-view padding on AI-estimated durations.** Buehler et al.'s planning-fallacy finding (actual time exceeded even subjects' own "worst case" estimate, topic 14) is ROBUST and directly actionable: pad AI schedule estimates using the operator's own historical estimate-vs-actual ratio, not the AI's fresh guess.

### Five fashionable ideas the evidence does not support

1. **"Protect the operator's willpower for hard decisions."** Ego-depletion as a resource-drain mechanism failed two large, independent, pre-registered multi-lab replications (d≈0.05, topic 13a). Do not architect scheduling logic around it.

2. **"Add an explanation and the operator will trust/verify appropriately."** Explanations reliably increase acceptance of AI output, not accuracy of the resulting decision (topic 9) — and can increase overreliance rather than reduce it absent a forcing function (topic 10).

3. **"Streaks and gamification are proven to drive engagement by X%."** The specific percentages circulating for Duolingo-style streaks and AI auto-scheduler productivity (Motion, Reclaim) are vendor marketing claims with no located methodology (topics 12, 18c) — SPECULATIVE, not evidence. The underlying psychology (loss aversion) is real; the numbers are not verified.

4. **"Progressive disclosure is settled UX science."** It is a reasonable, widely-adopted design convention with thin controlled-experiment backing (topic 6) — treat it as a hypothesis to validate with StateNour's own data, not a citation-ready fact.

5. **"If the operator feels the AI is helping, it is."** Two 2025 studies converge against this: users cannot reliably detect AI errors even at matched confidence (topic 24b), and experienced developers using AI coding tools were measurably 19% slower while believing themselves 20% faster (METR, topic 24d) — self-assessed AI benefit can be wrong in sign, not just magnitude. Instrument outcomes; do not trust the operator's (or the AI's own) felt sense of speed-up.
