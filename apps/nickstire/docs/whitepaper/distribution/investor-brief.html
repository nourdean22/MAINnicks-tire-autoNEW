How to Diligence an "AI-Enabled SMB" Claim

Three questions, answered against a live production system — Nick's Tire & Auto (Cleveland, OH)
Most "AI-enabled SMB" pitches show a demo. A demo proves a model works once, in front of an audience. It proves nothing about production discipline, guardrail architecture, or whether the metrics on the slide are real. This is the tool: three questions that separate a working system from a good demo — each answered here against a real single-location shop running this in production, not a lab.

The three questions

1 · Where's the verification loop?
Ask: how does the team know the system is working — not just that it runs?
What Nick's shows: a dashboard reported a 16.6% call-abandon rate — an alarming number that invited spending. A diagnostic first: ~81% of those "abandons" were sub-1-second robocall hang-ups, not lost customers, while AI first-token latency was a healthy ~244ms. The metric was an artifact. No spend happened until it was measured. A team with no verification loop cannot tell you this — they'll just show you the demo again.
2 · What can't happen by construction?
Ask: which failures are prevented by architecture, not by policy or good intentions?
What Nick's shows: every AI-handled call event is cryptographically signature-verified before it's trusted or logged. The self-learning loop can only reach the live customer-facing prompt after a lesson recurs across multiple days (confidence ≥0.65) and a human manually pushes the config — zero in 17 evaluation cycles reached the prompt. Business facts (phone, address, rating) are templated from code, not generated, so they cannot be hallucinated. "We have guardrails" is a policy claim. "The system cannot do X" is an architecture claim — ask for the second one.
3 · What number did the team lower on purpose?
Ask for one metric the team deliberately made worse, or stopped chasing, once they measured honestly.
What Nick's shows: a free domain-authority check (0 vs. page-1 incumbents at 66–94) proved organic search was structurally unwinnable — so SEO investment stopped entirely rather than escalating. AI-driven ROI is reported as a 40–70% capture band, not a point-estimate dollar figure, specifically so it can't be quietly inflated. A team that only ever reports numbers going up hasn't been tested yet.

Red flags this framework catches

Patterns worth a direct follow-up question, regardless of company:
  • ROI reported as a single dollar figure, with no stated method for how it's derived or bounded.
  • No described failure mode for the AI system — every team has one; a team that says "none" hasn't looked.
  • Guardrails described as configuration a customer or operator could accidentally turn off, rather than something the code enforces.
  • A metric that only ever improves, quarter over quarter, with no visible measurement methodology behind it.
